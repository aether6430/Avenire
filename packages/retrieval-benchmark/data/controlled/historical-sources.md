# Historical reasoning: primary and secondary sources

## Categories depend on the question

A primary source was created by a participant or observer during the period under study. A secondary source analyzes evidence after the events and may synthesize multiple primary sources. These labels describe a source's relationship to a research question, not an intrinsic badge of reliability. A textbook is secondary evidence for an ancient battle, but the same textbook could become a primary source in a study of how schools taught national history in the year it was published.

Letters, photographs, court records, newspapers, oral testimony, and material objects can all serve as primary evidence. Each was produced for a purpose. A tax register may record property consistently while omitting people outside the taxable system. A private letter may reveal personal language while exaggerating events for its intended reader. Administrative precision in one field does not guarantee completeness in another.

Secondary works also vary. A careful synthesis makes its sources and interpretive steps visible. A later publication is not automatically inferior to a contemporary account; it may compare archives unavailable to any single participant, recognize propaganda, or apply a chronology established by subsequent research.

## Source criticism

Source criticism asks who created a record, for what audience, under which constraints, and how its claims compare with independent evidence. Proximity to an event does not guarantee accuracy.

An eyewitness may have occupied a poor vantage point, misunderstood what was happening, remembered it years later, or shaped a story for legal and political reasons. Conversely, distance does not automatically disqualify a source. Historians evaluate the chain between event, record, preservation, and present interpretation.

Silence must be interpreted cautiously. The absence of a topic from an official report could mean it did not occur, but it could also reflect censorship, routine omission, lost pages, or a genre that never recorded such matters. Researchers should not convert “not mentioned here” directly into “did not happen.”

## Corroboration and dependence

The benchmark identifier `CORROBORATE_INDEPENDENT_02` marks the practice of comparing accounts that do not merely copy one another. Ten newspapers repeating one wire report provide less independent corroboration than their count suggests. Textual similarities, shared errors, timing, and publication networks can reveal dependence.

Genuine disagreement is also evidence. Conflicting records may expose differences in position, vocabulary, incentives, or jurisdiction. The historian's task is not always to collapse them into one neutral sentence; sometimes the conflict itself is the phenomenon that needs explaining. A defensible conclusion identifies both what the sources support and where uncertainty remains.
